How the map becomes sound
  • The map is coloured like NASA's SVS 5207 temperature map: blue is cooler than 1951–1980, white is normal, and red is warmer. Each band of latitude uses NASA GISTEMP's real zonal average for that year, so you can watch the Arctic heat up fastest. With the Python backend running, NASA's own SVS 5207 frames are shown instead.
  • One number drives the sound: the map's global temperature. Cooler means birdsong, crickets, frogs, waves and a gentle music-box tune. As it warms, the birds fade away completely, pollution surges (traffic, engines, horns, sirens, jackhammers, smog), the wind turns gusty, and the music climbs higher and gets tense.
  • Narrator: turn it on (top right, or press V) to hear the story, the year, the temperature and what's changing, read aloud. The other sounds get quieter while it speaks.
  • Keyboard: Space play/pause · ← → a year · N next chapter · M mute · V narrator.

Facts in the story, year by year

  • 1880: NASA's temperature record starts in 1880, painted on one map by SVS 5207: NASA GISS Surface Temperature Analysis (GISTEMP v4); NASA Scientific Visualization Studio, global temperature anomaly visualization SVS 5207
  • 1896: Arrhenius calculates warming from doubled carbon dioxide (about 6 °C): Svante Arrhenius, “On the Influence of Carbonic Acid in the Air upon the Temperature of the Ground”, Philosophical Magazine (1896)
  • 1908: the Model T and mass-produced cars: The Henry Ford, “Ford's Model T: A Car for the Great Multitude”
  • 1908: about 19.6 lb (8,887 g) of CO₂ per gallon of gasoline: U.S. EPA, “Greenhouse Gas Emissions from a Typical Passenger Vehicle”
  • 1912: the newspaper warning, and coal releasing billions of tons of CO₂ a year: The Spinoff, “The New Zealand newspaper climate report making waves 110 years later” (about the Rodney & Otamatea Times, 14 August 1912, on Papers Past)
  • 1938: Callendar links rising CO₂ to warming: G. S. Callendar, “The artificial production of carbon dioxide and its influence on temperature”, Quarterly Journal of the Royal Meteorological Society (1938)
  • 1950: the Great Acceleration: Steffen et al., “The trajectory of the Anthropocene: The Great Acceleration”, The Anthropocene Review (2015)
  • 1958: Keeling's measurements on Mauna Loa, starting near 315 ppm: Scripps Institution of Oceanography, “The Keeling Curve”; NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory, “Trends in Atmospheric Carbon Dioxide”
  • 1962: Silent Spring and the environmental movement: American Chemical Society, “Legacy of Rachel Carson's Silent Spring”
  • 1970: the first Earth Day and the founding of the EPA: U.S. EPA, “The Guardian: Origins of the EPA”
  • 1979: the Charney Report's 3 °C, give or take 1.5 °C: National Research Council, “Carbon Dioxide and Climate: A Scientific Assessment” (1979)
  • 1988: Hansen's Senate testimony: Pulitzer Center, transcript of James Hansen's testimony to the U.S. Senate, 23 June 1988 (PDF)
  • 1988: the IPCC is set up: IPCC, “History of the IPCC”
  • 1988: one of the warmest years recorded up to then: NASA GISS Surface Temperature Analysis (GISTEMP v4)
  • 1997: the Kyoto Protocol's emission targets: UNFCCC, “What is the Kyoto Protocol?”
  • 1998: El Niño boosts global temperatures: NASA Science, “The Ocean and Climate Change”
  • 1998: coral reefs suffer in the 1997–98 El Niño: NASA JPL, “Earth's Coral Reefs in Danger”
  • 2003: Europe's heatwave and its death toll (more than 70,000): Robine et al., “Death toll exceeded 70,000 in Europe during the summer of 2003”, Comptes Rendus Biologies (2008)
  • 2003: heatwaves getting hotter and more frequent: NASA Science, “The Effects of Climate Change”
  • 2015: the Paris Agreement, well below 2 °C and aiming for 1.5 °C: UNFCCC, “Key aspects of the Paris Agreement”
  • 2016 and 2023: record warm years, most of the hottest since 2015; 2023 the warmest: NASA, “NASA Analysis Confirms 2023 as Warmest Year on Record” (2024)
  • 2019: about 3 billion fewer birds in North America since 1970 (about 29%): Rosenberg et al., “Decline of the North American avifauna”, Science (2019)
  • 2023: CO₂ about 421 ppm today and 280 ppm before the Industrial Revolution: NOAA Climate.gov, “Climate Change: Atmospheric Carbon Dioxide”; NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory

Quick facts

  • The map colours: blue cooler, white normal, red warmer: NASA Scientific Visualization Studio, global temperature anomaly visualization SVS 5207
  • The Arctic is warming much faster than the rest of the Earth: NOAA, “Arctic Report Card: Update for 2025”
  • Oceans have soaked up about 90% of the extra heat: NASA Science, “Ocean Warming” Earth indicator
  • Hotter air holds more water, so more heavy rain and flooding: NASA Science, “The Effects of Climate Change”
  • Birds shifting their ranges toward the poles and uphill: NASA Science, “The Effects of Climate Change”
  • Each SVS 5207 frame averages 24 months: NASA Scientific Visualization Studio, global temperature anomaly visualization SVS 5207
  • A fraction of a degree is a big deal for the whole planet: NASA Science, “Why a half-degree temperature rise is a big deal”
  • Spring songs and chirps arriving earlier: Audubon, “A Matter of Timing: Can Birds Keep Up With Earlier and Earlier Springs?”
  • 1.5 °C of warming is far less harmful than 2 °C: IPCC, “Special Report: Global Warming of 1.5 °C” (2018)

The ending

  • Warming of about 1.1 °C, seas up about 8 inches since 1880, Arctic summers nearly ice-free before 2050: NASA Science, “The Effects of Climate Change”

Sounds and music

  • Every sound in The Warming Song (birds, wind, rivers, traffic, sirens, factories, music and the whoosh) is generated live by this project's own code using the Web Audio API, an open web standard built into every browser (W3C Web Audio API). No recordings, samples or music by anyone else are used, so there is nothing copyrighted to license.
  • Narration: voiced by Dilyana Dobreva, who is over 18 and gives NASA consent to use her voice recordings. If a recording can't load, a free open-source backup voice reads the line instead: Piper text-to-speech (MIT licence) with the “Norman” voice, trained on public-domain LibriVox recordings (voice details), running in the browser with ONNX Runtime Web (MIT), piper-tts-web (MIT) and eSpeak NG (GPL-3.0).
